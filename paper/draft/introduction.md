# Introduction

Cooperation sustained by learning depends on both current choices and the information retained from earlier interactions. In a public goods game, the observed population fractions describe how many agents contribute, defect, or punish. They do not reveal how close an unchosen action is to becoming preferred. Even a complete specification of an agent's current action-selection probabilities need not determine its response to subsequent learning: different action values can produce the same policy before an update and different policies afterward. The empirical question within a simulation model is how large this dependence becomes, which branches of the dynamics generate it, and whether it extends to values produced by plausible learning histories.

The public goods model of @zou2026 combines cooperation, defection, and peer punishment on uniform random hypergraphs with self-regarding Q-learning. It provides a concrete setting in which collective contribution and punishment can coexist without agents directly imitating successful neighbors. We retain its payoff and learning structure. Our question concerns an intervention on the learned state rather than a new cooperation-promoting rule: can initially unexpressed differences in punishment values alter subsequent shock responses while the entire initial policy remains fixed?

This distinction requires care in defining both the treatment and the outcome. A shock changes behavior at the intervention time, but a modification of action values can also change the trajectory that would have occurred without that shock. Comparing shocked trajectories alone conflates these two responses; comparing shock-induced losses alone can conceal a deteriorating unshocked reference. In a different trust-game setting, @guo2026 compare feedback branches initialized from a common learned state and distinguish absolute outcomes from paired contrasts. We adopt that paired-comparison perspective without importing their environmental feedback mechanism into the present model.

We construct a gap intervention that changes strictly suboptimal punishment values and preserves the greedy action set in every row of every agent's Q-table. With fixed exploration, this also preserves the full initial policy. The construction yields a pathwise prediction: if all subsequent Q-updates are disabled, intervention branches subjected to the same shock and random events must behave identically. When learning continues, paired shocked and unshocked branches measure the intervention's effect on a fixed-window incremental loss. The distinction between Q-values and policies is inherent in value-based learning [@watkins1992]; the purpose of the experiment is to quantify and audit its collective consequences in this model, rather than to claim that this distinction is itself new.

The analysis has three parts. First, a fixed-design experiment estimates the controlled gap contrast on networks that were not used for operating-point selection or variance planning. Second, absolute-path decomposition, frozen-learning controls, matched-action interventions, and event-level replays delimit its interpretation. Third, natural-history experiments test whether comparable gap values can be obtained from different punishment-cost histories and transplanted while preserving the recipient policy. The last step separates insufficient support from an estimable but uncertain effect. The resulting account is deliberately narrower than a general claim that hidden learning memory improves resilience: the controlled contrast is nonzero, but its practical magnitude, baseline dependence, action specificity, and natural-history interpretation must be considered separately.
