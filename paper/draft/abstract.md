Agents with the same current behavior and the same initial action-selection policy can retain different action values. We examine whether these differences alter shock responses in a public goods game with punishment and asynchronous Q-learning on uniform hypergraphs. At fixed learned checkpoints, we rescale strictly suboptimal punishment-value gaps while preserving every node's policy in every state. Three intervention levels are crossed with shocked and unshocked continuations using shared random events. Across 20 independent networks, two histories per network, and ten paired futures per history, the near-minus-far difference in incremental cooperation loss is 1.068 percentage points (95% network-bootstrap interval: 1.018--1.118), below the prespecified two-point relevance threshold. Absolute trajectories show that much of this contrast reflects a transient decline in the far intervention's unshocked branch. Freezing all Q-values eliminates intervention-dependent behavioral differences. Eight prespecified replay prefixes identify a local onset mechanism: decreasing cooperation values expose unchanged punishment values as greedy choices. Matched-action controls do not establish punishment specificity. Long high-cost histories lack sufficient matching support, whereas a separately calibrated short-cost pulse permits transplantation of naturally observed gaps; its effect intervals include zero at all three washout times. These findings establish a controlled dependence on hidden action values at one operating point, while limiting claims about natural learning histories and improved resilience.
